Hello Padel research enthusiasts!

In high-level amateur padel, the players who lost moved more per minute than the players who won, and each side of the court moved in its own way. A second padel study found that 59.1% of points in elite junior semi-finals and finals ended on an error.

Away from the court, a new review of 15 studies and 9,002 people found no sign that regular stretching programmes lower injury risk. In contrast to that, a review of 27 studies found that neuromuscular warm-up programmes were linked to 28% lower risk of leg injuries.

Each week I sift through the new studies on padel, fitness, nutrition and recovery, and pull out what is worth using on court for us as coaches and players. This week, two new padel papers, an injury prevention reset and a tournament nutrition tip jumped out. Here's my take on them.

## Three key points from the research this week

- In high-level amateur padel, the players who lost moved more per minute than the players who won.
- Stretching did not prevent injuries but short neuromuscular warm-ups, 2 or 3 times a week, did better.
- On a rest day, thinking about the last match was linked to more mental fatigue, and the fatigue fed more rumination.

## New padel research

### Losers moved more & each side of the court moves differently

Researchers put inertial sensors on 12 high-level amateur men (aged 18 to 25) across one official tournament, giving 45 match records. Per minute of play, the players who lost had a higher Player Load (a measure of total body movement, d = -1.00) and more impacts (d = -0.69) than the winners. Distance, accelerations, decelerations and jumps did not differ.

Right-side players made more forward and leftward changes of direction. Left-side players made more backward and backward-right changes of direction. In every group, load dropped from set 1 to set 2 ([Granados et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.3390/app162211498)).

**What I take from it:** Running more is not the same as playing better, so I would look at court position before fitness when a pair is chasing every ball. And I would train footwork for each player's side: backward and diagonal recovery for the left, forward and lateral for the right.

**Caveats:** Small sample, men only, one tournament.

### Most points end on a mistake

A second study coded 915 point-ending shots from six semi-finals and finals of the 2024 Junior Master Final, played by 16 elite Under-18 players. 59.1% of points ended with an error and 40.9% with a winner. When a point ended on a smash, it was a winner 86.2% of the time. Forehand volleys were winners 56.8% of the time and bandeja or víbora 48.7%. Lobs, side-wall shots and backhand volleys ended in errors most often. The pairs who won their matches did not end more points with winners (39.8% vs 42.1%) ([Sánchez-Alcaraz et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.3390/app162211490)).

**What I take from it:** Matches are lost more than they are won. In training I would reward the safe ball that keeps you at the net, and save the smash for the ball that is really there.

**Caveats:** These are junior players and only the last shot of each point was coded, so treat it as a pattern, not a rule for adults.

## Injury prevention

### Stretching did not lower injury risk

This is the largest stretching review in years: over 300,000 records screened, 19 studies kept and 15 pooled, with 9,002 people aged 14 to 59. Most programmes were static stretching for 4 to 24 weeks. Stretching made no clear difference to injury risk against any comparison (odds ratio 0.945, 95% CI 0.828 to 1.078). No effect showed up for muscle, tendon or ligament injuries either. No study tested stretching just before play ([Afonso et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-026-02559-5)).

**What I take from it:** Stretch if you want more range or it helps you feel loose. But if you have 15 minutes for injury prevention, stretching is not where I would spend them.

Most trials had a high risk of bias and over 80% of participants were men.

### Short neuromuscular warm-ups were linked to fewer leg injuries

Neuromuscular training means warm-up programmes built on balance, landing, strength and agility drills. Across 27 trials and 27,663 athletes, these programmes were linked to a 28% lower risk of lower-limb injury (risk ratio 0.72). Programmes run 2 to 3 times a week, for 16 to 25 minutes, looked best, but that dose finding is low certainty ([Gu et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40798-026-01142-6)).

**What I take from it:** This is where the 15 minutes go. Single-leg balance, landing drills, a few strength moves and some quick footwork, twice a week before you play.

**Caveats:** Most trials were in football and basketball, and overall certainty was very low.

### Extra motor drills helped tennis elbow (a little)

In 43 adults with long-standing tennis elbow, everyone did 6 weeks of wrist extensor strengthening and stretching. Half also did motor learning drills twice a week. That group gained 4.9 kg more grip strength and had a little less pain at rest, but function scores and pain during activity were no different ([Gercek et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jht.2026.09.028)).

**What I take from it:** For elbow pain, the loading programme does most of the work. Both groups improved on every measure.

**Caveats:** The extra group also got more total treatment time.

## Fitness on court

### 20 minutes a week of reaction footwork is enough to improve

45 athletes, 19 from open-skill sports (where you react to an opponent e.g. padel) and 26 from closed-skill sports, did one 20-minute reactive agility session a week for 6 weeks. Reactive agility means moving to a signal you cannot predict. Both groups got faster on a reactive agility test and a change of direction test, and scored better on a thinking speed test (all p < 0.05). The open-skill athletes started faster but improved at the same rate ([Laukhardt et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsc.70297)).

**What I take from it:** One short block a week works. Have a partner point or call a direction, and the player moves, splits and recovers to the net.

**Caveats:** There was no control group, so some of the gain may be practice at the tests.

## Nutrition

### Beetroot juice gives a small edge, but only over several days

A review of 24 trials with 365 team-sport athletes (88 of them women) looked at dietary nitrate, usually taken as beetroot juice. The overall effect was small (g = 0.075). Sport-specific tasks improved a little more (g = 0.143). Taking it for 2 days or more helped (g = 0.145), while a single dose showed no clear effect. Single sprints did not improve ([Szymocha et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1080/15502783.2026.2751378)).

**What I take from it:** If a player wants to try it, start a few days before a tournament, not on the morning of the final. Expect a small gain at best.

## The mental side and recovery

### Replaying the match on a rest day keeps you tired

134 competitive athletes reported rumination (going over the same thoughts again and again) and mental fatigue on rest days for four weeks. The two fed each other in a loop, and the path from rumination to fatigue was the stronger one. Athletes who were very self-critical were more caught in the loop ([Aleixo et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psychsport.2026.103282)).

**What I take from it:** A rest day spent replaying a lost tie-break is not full rest. I would give players one simple routine: write three lines about the match, close the notebook, and do something else.

**Caveat:** This was self-report and observational, so it shows a link, not a cause.

### After a late away match, the first night is bad either way

19 Premier League players were tracked over 10 European away games. Sleep quality dropped on match night and the night after. Staying overnight meant 1.94 hours less sleep on match night than flying home, but players who played then slept 1.45 hours more the next night. Flying home gave no rebound ([Thorpe et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/19417381261502203)).

**What I take from it:** After a late match at an away tournament, plan a poor first night and keep the next day light.

## Three things to try this week

1. Swap 10 minutes of static stretching for a neuromuscular warm-up: single-leg balance, soft landings, a few lunges and quick footwork, before 2 or 3 sessions this week.
2. Add one 20-minute reaction block: a partner calls or points, you split-step, move and recover to your side of the net.
3. Before your next tournament, if you want to try beetroot juice, start it a few days out. After each match, write three lines about it so you can switch off.
