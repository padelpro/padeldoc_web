Padel is booming here. Courts going up everywhere, Playtomic filling up, companies rolling out courts all over Ireland. But for all the growth, almost nobody has measured how the game is actually played here. Until now.

A new study by Taylor Brandon, Dr Kevin O'Brien and Dr Shane Mangan from TUD took eight competitive matches from clubs around Dublin and broke down 1,008 rallies, shot by shot, to answer one simple question: at our level, what actually separates the pairs who win from the pairs who lose? It looked at sub-elite men's padel (players rated 3.0 and up on Playtomic), so it is not the pro tour and it is not the whole country. But it is the first proper look at the Irish game, and there is plenty in it for any club player.

## Winners and losers are playing a similar game

Here is the surprise. The pairs who won and the pairs who lost looked almost identical. Same shot choices, same court positions, same shot directions. What set them apart was not what they did, but how well they did it.

## The winning points are forced, not gifted

Breaking down how points were actually won, two of the three outcomes tell the same story. Direct winners (about 22% of all points) and forced errors (about 38%) are essentially the same thing: the attacking pair imposing itself, forcing a shot the other pair could not handle. Put them together and close to 60% of points came from one pair making something happen, not from a soft mistake. Only the unforced errors (about 40%) were true presents. That is higher than in elite padel, where unforced errors account for roughly 30% of points won and lost.

That is where the winners pulled ahead. Weigh the aggressive outcomes (winners plus forced errors) against the presents (unforced errors), and the winning pairs came out at 1.8, against 1.1 for the losers. In plain terms, they forced almost twice as much as they gave away, while the losing pairs barely tipped the balance. It was the one thing statistically linked to winning (p = 0.005), though the effect was small (Conde-Ripoll et al., 2024; Escudero-Tena et al., 2021).

So the winning game at this level is an aggressive one. And two things carry that aggression, both with plenty of room to grow in Ireland: the net, and the overhead.

## The net is where Irish padel has the most to gain

In elite men's padel, the large majority of winning shots are made from attacking positions up at the net, somewhere around 80% across the published studies. At Irish sub-elite level it was a different picture. Far fewer winners were struck from right up at the net (about 29%), with a large share still coming from the baseline (40%). Studies draw their zone lines differently, so treat the exact gap with care, but net play looks less embedded in how the game is played here yet (Ungureanu et al., 2022).

In elite men's padel, the single most common way a point ends is with a clean winner, somewhere around 38 to 44% depending on the study, while unforced errors are around 30% (Conde-Ripoll et al., 2024; Sánchez-Alcaraz et al., 2021). Our game looks like the mirror image. In this study the most common single outcome was an unforced error (40.5%), and clean winners made up just 21.9%. Put simply, elite padel is won by finishing, and the sub-elite Irish game is too often lost by gifting. The pairs who close that gap, by cutting out the cheap mistakes and actually putting the ball away, are the ones who will drag the Irish standard up.

That is not a criticism, it is an opportunity. The biggest gap between our game and the elite game looks like net occupation, and possibly effectiveness at the net. The pairs who learn to get to the net, hold it, and volley with control will be doing something most of their opponents simply are not. This is the clearest path to a better standard of Irish padel.

## The overhead is how points get finished

Getting to the net is only half of it. You have to be able to put the ball away when you get there, and that means overheads. The winning pairs in this study had one finishing pattern above all others: an opponent lobs, and they punish it with a bandeja or víbora (Conde-Ripoll et al., 2024).

The bandeja, the víbora and the smash are the shots that end rallies. Most winning shots also landed in the opponent's transition zone (around 62%), the hardest spot on the court to defend from (Martín-Miguel et al., 2024; Courel-Ibáñez et al., 2019). Read the lob early, move to it, and finish with the overhead. Do that consistently and your level climbs with it.

## What this means for your game

The to-do list is short and very doable:

1. **Cut the unforced errors.** They are the points you gift away, and the one thing fully in your control.
2. **Get to the net and learn to hold it.** Most pairs at this level still do not.
3. **Sharpen your overheads.** The bandeja, víbora and smash are how points get finished.
4. **Work your lob and punish theirs.** Forcing a lob to overhead is the winning pattern.

## The fine print

This is one small pilot study: only eight matches, all around Dublin, all men's pairs, looking only at the last three shots of each point. Most of the differences were not statistically significant, so treat it as a first benchmark and not the final word. Two more cautions. Where one coder draws the line between a winner and a forced error will not match every published study, so the combined figure (winners plus forced errors, about 60%) is safer to compare than winners alone. And the court zones used here are not identical to those in the elite studies, so the net and transition comparisons are a pointer, not an exact measure.

But that is the point of this study. To be the first. Nobody had measured how padel is actually played in Ireland before, and the message is an encouraging one. You do not need elite tactics to move the Irish game forward. Get to the net, finish with your overhead, and force more points than you give away. Simple to say. Worth practising. And exactly how Irish padel takes its next step.
