From a recent research study, 50% of padel players received an injury - and the majority in the upper limbs.

Every Monday I go through the new studies in padel, racket sports, strength and conditioning, nutrition and recovery. I keep the ones that matter for club and competitive players and I give you my synopsis from them. Here's the most important ones so far.

**The week in three bullet points**

- New padel data: shoulder, elbow and wrist injuries lead, and more hours on court go with more ankle and knee problems - as expected? What do you think?
- Caffeine: a small dose (3 mg per kg) already sharpens sprint, jump and reaction time.
- Short sprint intervals on court beat long steady runs for fitness, footwork and stroke quality (tennis).

## New padel research

### Where padel players get hurt

A survey of 600 players in Saudi Arabia found that 50% had picked up at least one padel injury, with 513 injuries reported in total ([Albesher et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/10538127261506062)).

| Area | Share of injuries |
| --- | --- |
| Shoulder, elbow, wrist and hand | 44% |
| Foot and ankle | 22% |
| Back | 19% |
| Knee | 15% |

Players who played more hours each week had more ankle and knee injuries. Racket hardness and whether players had been coached were also linked to where they got hurt.

**What I take from it:** the arm takes the biggest hit, so shoulder and forearm strength work earns its place in your week. If you add court time, add it gradually. And if your elbow is grumbling, look at your racket before you blame your technique alone.

This is a self-report survey, so it shows patterns, not causes.

### Can you trust the stats from a padel app?

A team in Murcia compared an automated padel stats app with careful frame-by-frame video coding over three amateur matches ([Sánchez-Alcaraz et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.3389/fspor.2026.1915133)). The app showed 15% fewer shots overall: 45% fewer smashes, 28% fewer forehands and 20% fewer backhands, but 27% more forehand volleys than were really hit.

**What I take from it:** app stats are fine for a broad picture of your match. Do not change your game plan on shot-by-shot numbers until you have checked them against the video.

Three matches and one player is a small test, so treat this as a warning, not a verdict. I have written this one up in full in a separate post: [Can You Trust Your Padel App's Stats?](/research/2026-11-17-padel-app-stats)

## Caffeine: how much is enough?

Two new trials point the same way: a moderate dose does most of the work.

In 21 trained sprinters, 3 mg of caffeine per kg of body weight cut 60 m time by 0.12 s and improved reaction time, jump height and power ([Mei et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0361864)). Doubling to 6 mg/kg added a little more speed and jump, but no extra gain in reaction time.

In 15 elite female volleyball players, caffeine gum helped them hold their jump and reaction speed after a hard fatigue block ([Zhong et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1080/15502783.2026.2749890)). Without caffeine, their reaction sequence slowed by 18%. With 6 mg/kg it slowed by only 3%. The higher dose also brought more side effects.

**What I take from it:** for a 75 kg player, 3 mg/kg is about 225 mg, roughly two espressos, taken about an hour before you play. Gum works faster if you need a lift late in a long match. Try it in training first, never for the first time at a tournament, and skip it for evening matches if it wrecks your sleep.

## Fitness on court

### Sprint intervals beat long runs

48 college tennis players trained three times a week for 8 weeks, either with court sprints (110 m of shuttle runs, 3 sets of 3, 20 s rest) or with 30 to 60 minute steady runs ([Li et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2026.1956423)). The sprint group gained more aerobic fitness (VO2max up 2.8 vs 1.8 ml/kg/min). They also improved more in sprint speed, change of direction, reactive jumping and stroke accuracy.

**My take-home message?** You do not need long runs to get fit for padel. Short, hard shuttles on court do more, in less time.

### A morning primer for an afternoon match

25 rugby players did 3 sets of 3 heavy squats plus 5 sets of 3 jump squats in the morning. Five and a half hours later they were faster over 40 m (0.12 s) and jumped higher than on a rest morning ([Knight et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsc.70286)).

**What I take from it:** if you have a late match and access to a gym, a short heavy and explosive session that morning may help. Test it on a training day first.

### Tired legs change how you lunge

In 24 badminton players, fatigue made lunges stiffer, with less push to recover back to position ([Xie et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.3389/fbioe.2026.1967879)). Backhand-side lunges put more load through the ankle than forehand ones.

**What I take from it:** practise your split step, lunge and recovery step at the end of sessions, when you are tired, not only when fresh. Give your backhand-side ankle some extra strength and balance work.

### Read the shot earlier

Badminton players who trained with videos cut off earlier and earlier before the shot got better at reading where it was going ([Gao et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.2001694)).

**What I take from it:** pause match videos just before your opponent hits and call the shot. It is a cheap way to train your reading of lobs and drop shots.

## Injury and recovery

### A simple knee check for club players

628 recreational athletes aged 18 to 50 were followed for a year, and 15% injured a knee ([Rivarola et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/23259671261459787)). The warning signs were simple: age 35 or over, carrying extra weight, a previous knee injury, failing a single-leg squat, playing while very tired, and skipping the warm-up.

**What I take from it:** most of those you can check or change yourself. Film a single-leg squat on each side. If your knee drops inwards, work on it. Never skip the warm-up, and think twice about playing on a day you feel wrecked.

### Tennis elbow: train the shoulder blade too

A review of 12 studies found that adding shoulder blade strength work to normal physio improved arm function more than physio alone ([Rais et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jht.2026.09.013)). It did not clearly reduce pain.

**What I take from it:** if you have elbow pain, rows and shoulder blade exercises belong alongside your forearm work. See a physio for the full plan.

### Sore after a hard weekend?

In 40 university athletes, light isometric holds (pushing against a fixed resistance without moving) cut muscle soreness the day after hard exercise by about 2.5 points on a 10-point scale ([Kurtulmuş et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jsams.2026.10.018)). Strength did not come back any faster.

**What I take from it:** a few easy wall-sit or leg-press holds the day after a tournament can take the edge off. It is a comfort tool, not a fast track back to full power.

## Hydration and the mental side

### Add protein to your recovery drink

A review of 16 studies found that drinks containing protein (about 24 g per litre) kept more fluid in the body than drinks without it ([Gibbons et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-026-02577-3)). People peed out less and topped up their blood volume more.

**What I take from it:** after a hot match, or between rounds with a long gap, milk or a protein shake rehydrates you and starts your recovery in one go. Most of the studies were on men, so we need more data on women.

### Mental tiredness costs you in the gym

After 45 minutes of a draining mental task, trained lifters managed 4 to 9 fewer squat reps across four sets ([Dello Stritto et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1123/ijspp.2025-0638)).

**What I take from it:** a long day at a screen will take something off your gym session. On those days, lower the target or move the heavy work.

### Nerves: try acceptance, not just pep talks

80 elite taekwondo athletes did 8 weeks of either mindfulness and acceptance training or classic mental skills training ([Gao et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.actpsy.2026.108102)). The mindfulness group felt less anxious, more confident and switched tasks faster, and the gains held three months later.

**What I take from it:** learning to notice nerves and play anyway may work better than trying to talk yourself out of them. That fits padel, where you need to change plans mid-point.

## Three things to try this week

1. Swap one long run for court shuttles: 3 sets of 3, with 20 s between reps and 5 minutes between sets.
2. Film a single-leg squat on each leg. If your knee caves in, start working on it.
3. If you use caffeine, test 3 mg per kg in training before your next match.
